## Why most notes don't stick

For a long time my notes were just copied definitions. I'd write down what attention mechanisms are, or how gradient descent works, and feel productive. Then a week later I couldn't explain any of it out loud. The notes existed, but the understanding didn't.

The problem was that I was transcribing, not processing. Copying a slide is not the same as learning the idea behind it.

## What I changed

Now every note I take during a lecture, paper, or course follows the same small structure.

- What is the concept, in one plain sentence, written in my own words.
- Why does it exist. What problem was someone trying to solve when they invented it.
- Where was I confused, and did I resolve it or not.
- One small example I made up myself, not copied from the source.

That last point matters most. If I can't invent my own example, I don't actually understand the concept yet. I just recognize it.

## Linking notes together

I keep a running file of short summaries, and whenever a new concept connects to an older one, I write that link explicitly. Backpropagation connects to gradient descent. Tokenization connects to how transformers see text. Over time this turns into a map instead of a pile of disconnected facts, and it's much easier to recall something when you remember what it's attached to.

## Reviewing on a schedule

I revisit notes a few days later, not the same day. If I can still explain the concept without rereading it, I mark it as solid. If I struggle, I rewrite the explanation again, usually shorter than before. The act of compressing an idea is often where real understanding happens, more than the first time you encounter it.

This system isn't fancy. It's just honest about what I actually remember versus what I think I remember, and that gap is where most learning gets lost.
