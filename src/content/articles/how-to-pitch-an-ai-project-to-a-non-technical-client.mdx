## Talk about their problem, not your tech stack

Early at Codexier AB, I used to walk into client meetings ready to explain how a model worked. I quickly learned that almost nobody in the room cared. They cared about whether their invoices got processed faster, whether their support queue got shorter, or whether they could stop manually copying data between two systems.

Now I open every pitch with the problem in their words, not mine. If I cannot describe their pain in one sentence without using the word "AI," I am not ready to pitch yet.

## Show one concrete example

Abstract capability lists do not land. Saying "we can automate document processing using machine learning" means very little to someone running a small logistics business. Showing them one real document, what currently happens to it, and what would happen with the new system, lands immediately.

- Use their own data or a close approximation of it.
- Walk through the before and after side by side.
- Be specific about what a human still needs to check.

## Be upfront about risk

Non-technical clients are often more worried about the AI being confidently wrong than about it being slow. I always spend a few minutes on what happens when the system makes a mistake, who catches it, and how much that costs. Clients trust you more when you bring up the failure case before they ask about it.

## Price the maintenance, not just the build

One thing I had to learn directly: clients budget for the build and forget that AI systems need ongoing attention as data and behavior shift. I now always present a build cost and a much smaller, clearly explained ongoing cost, so there are no surprises six months in.

## The simple version

Pitch the outcome, prove it with one real example, name the risk honestly, and price the whole lifecycle. That combination has won me more trust than any slide about model architecture ever did.
