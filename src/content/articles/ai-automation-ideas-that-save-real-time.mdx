## Automate the draft, not the decision

Running Codexier AB and helping with Forare at the same time taught me to be picky about what gets automated. The pattern that works well is automating the first draft of something and leaving a human to make the final call. That keeps risk low, because even if the AI output is imperfect, a person catches it before it goes anywhere important. The pattern that backfires is automating the decision itself in anything customer-facing or financial, because the cost of a quiet mistake is much higher there.

## Ideas that consistently save real time

- Drafting first responses to repetitive customer emails, which a person then edits and sends.
- Summarizing long meeting notes or call transcripts into a short list of action items.
- Turning rough bullet points into a first draft of a proposal, report, or social post.
- Sorting and tagging incoming requests or support tickets by topic, so nothing gets lost in a generic inbox.
- Generating a first pass of test cases or checklist items for a recurring process.

Each of these targets a task that happens often, takes real time, and is low-risk if the first draft needs editing.

## What to avoid automating early

I am cautious about automating anything where a wrong output is expensive or hard to notice, like final pricing decisions, legal language, or anything sent directly to a customer without review. These can be automated eventually, but only after the simpler, lower-stakes automations have proven the tool is reliable for your specific data and use case.

## How to tell if an automation is actually working

I track one simple number for every automation: hours saved per week, measured honestly against how long the task took before. If an automation does not show a clear time saving after a few weeks, it usually means the task was not repetitive enough to be worth automating, or the tool was not matched well to the task. Real automation wins are boring and measurable, not flashy.
