## Passing the exam isn't the same as solving the problem

I can explain how a random forest splits decision boundaries, what a learning rate does, and why batch normalization helps training stability. None of that automatically meant I could take a real dataset and produce something useful. There's a wide gap between recognizing a concept on a slide and applying it correctly to a problem nobody designed to be clean.

## Theory describes the happy path

Textbook examples are almost always well-behaved. The features are relevant, the labels are correct, the classes are roughly balanced. Real data rarely cooperates. Labels are inconsistent, important features are missing, and the thing you actually care about predicting is rare. Theory teaches you the mechanism. Application teaches you what happens when the assumptions behind that mechanism don't hold.

## Debugging is the real curriculum

The most educational moments for me weren't lectures, they were staring at a model that wasn't learning and trying to figure out why. Was it the data, the loss function, the learning rate, a bug in preprocessing? Theory gives you a list of suspects. Only practice teaches you how to investigate them in the right order, because in a real project you can't test everything at once.

## How I try to close the gap

Whenever I learn a new concept now, I try to apply it to something small within the same week, even if it's a toy dataset. The friction of actually implementing an idea surfaces questions that reading never would. I've learned more about regularization from watching my own model overfit than from any explanation of why overfitting happens.

## The honest takeaway

Theory is necessary, but it's the starting line, not the finish line. If you can explain a concept but have never watched it fail in your own hands, you don't fully own it yet.
