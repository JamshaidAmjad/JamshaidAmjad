## Models don't know things, they fit patterns

Early on I treated models like they understood the world. They don't. A model finds statistical patterns between inputs and outputs in the data it was shown. If the data is biased, incomplete, or narrow, the model's "knowledge" is exactly as biased, incomplete, or narrow. This single idea reframes almost everything else about AI. It's not a small intelligence in a box, it's a very advanced curve fit.

## Validation isn't a formality

I used to treat the validation set as a box to check before submitting an assignment. It's actually the only honest signal you have that your model will work on data it hasn't seen. Training accuracy tells you how well a model memorized. Validation accuracy tells you how well it generalized. Confusing the two is one of the most common beginner mistakes, and I made it constantly before someone explained the difference clearly.

## The data is almost always the real problem

I spent a long time thinking better architectures would fix bad results. Most of the time the fix was in the data: mislabeled examples, missing categories, leakage between train and test sets, or simply not having enough examples of the rare case that mattered most. Once I started treating data quality as the first thing to debug, my results improved faster than any architecture change ever did.

## Simpler models deserve more respect

I assumed deep learning was always the upgrade. Plenty of problems are solved better, faster, and more reliably by a simple regression or a decision tree. Complexity should be earned by the problem, not assumed by default.

## Why this matters

None of these ideas are exotic. They're the kind of thing that gets mentioned once in a lecture and then assumed forever after. If you're early in learning AI, it's worth slowing down on these basics before chasing the latest model architecture, because they explain why everything else behaves the way it does.
