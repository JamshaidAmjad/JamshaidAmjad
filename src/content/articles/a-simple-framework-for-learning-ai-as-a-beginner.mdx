## The problem with most learning paths

Most AI courses start with math: linear algebra, calculus, probability, and only later get to anything you can build. That order makes sense for a math degree. It doesn't make sense for someone trying to understand what AI can actually do. I switched the order and it changed everything for me.

## Stage one: intuition before formulas

Before touching equations, I try to answer one question in plain language: what is this technique actually trying to do, and why would someone need it. Gradient descent is just a way to nudge a model's parameters toward fewer mistakes. Once that idea is solid, the formula becomes a description of something you already understand, instead of a wall you have to memorize blindly.

## Stage two: build something small and ugly

After the intuition stage, I build the smallest possible version of the idea. Not a polished project, just enough code to watch the concept behave. A tiny script that trains a model on a toy dataset and prints the loss going down teaches you more than rereading a chapter. Things that seemed abstract, like overfitting or learning rate, become visible the moment you can watch numbers move.

## Stage three: go back to theory with a real question

Only after building something do I return to the deeper math or research papers, and at that point I have a specific question instead of a vague one. "Why did my loss explode" is a much better starting point for learning about learning rates than reading a textbook section in order.

## Why this order works

Doing things in this order means theory answers questions you actually have, instead of questions you're told to have. It also keeps motivation high, because you're building things from week one rather than waiting until you've "earned" the right to build something real.
