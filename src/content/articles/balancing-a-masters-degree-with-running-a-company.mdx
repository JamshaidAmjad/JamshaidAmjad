## It is an energy problem, not a time problem

When people ask how I balance a Master's in Applied AI with running Codexier AB, they usually assume it is about finding more hours in the day. It is not. The real constraint is mental energy. A morning spent debugging a client integration leaves a different kind of tired than three hours of coursework on optimization theory, and pretending I can switch between them freely was the first mistake I made.

## Fixed blocks beat flexible intentions

Early on, I tried to keep my schedule flexible so I could respond to whichever side needed attention. That flexibility quietly let coursework slip whenever a client had an urgent request, because client work always feels more immediate. What fixed it was protecting specific blocks of time for study, treated with the same seriousness as a client meeting, not as something I would get to if the week allowed it.

## Sweden's structure actually helped

Studying in Sweden, where course pacing tends to be deliberate rather than constantly compressed, gave me more room to plan study blocks in advance than I expected. I leaned into that structure instead of fighting it, mapping out exam and deadline weeks early and warning clients ahead of time that my availability would shrink, rather than discovering the conflict the week it happened.

## The two sides do feed each other

What surprised me is how often something from coursework, a concept in model evaluation or a paper on a specific architecture, directly improved a decision I made at Codexier AB days later. That only happens if you actually let the two worlds talk to each other instead of mentally filing them as separate. I now look for that overlap on purpose instead of treating study and company as competing obligations.

## What I would tell someone starting this

Decide in advance which one gets priority during a real conflict, before the conflict happens. For me, that is usually the degree, because it is time-boxed and the company is not going anywhere. Having that answer ready removes a decision I used to make badly under pressure.
