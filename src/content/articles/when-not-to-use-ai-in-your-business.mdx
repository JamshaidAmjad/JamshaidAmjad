## Saying no is part of the job

A meaningful part of running Codexier AB has been telling potential clients that they do not need AI for what they are describing. It is not the answer people expect from a software and AI services company, but it is usually the right one, and it builds far more trust than saying yes to everything.

## The clearest sign: a simple rule already works

If a problem can be solved with a clear, stable rule, like "flag any invoice over this amount" or "send a reminder after three days," a few lines of code will outperform a model. It will be cheaper to build, easier to debug, and it will not drift in behavior over time. I default to asking: is this actually a pattern-recognition problem, or is it a rule someone already knows and just has not written down.

## Volume matters more than people expect

AI systems carry a fixed cost to build and a real ongoing cost to monitor. If a task happens five times a month, that cost rarely pays for itself, no matter how impressive the automation would look. I have walked away from projects specifically because the volume did not justify the investment, even when the client wanted the shiny version.

## You need a real cost for being wrong

Before recommending AI for anything client-facing, I ask what a wrong answer actually costs. In some workflows, a wrong suggestion is mildly annoying and easy to correct. In others, like anything touching compliance, payments, or health information, a wrong answer is expensive or unsafe. If nobody on the client side can tell me what a mistake costs, that is a sign the problem is not ready for AI yet, regardless of how good the model is.

## The honest filter

My rule now: AI earns its place when the pattern is genuinely fuzzy, the volume is real, and someone has thought through what happens when it is wrong. Outside of that, a simpler tool almost always wins, and recommending it instead of AI is usually the more professional answer.
