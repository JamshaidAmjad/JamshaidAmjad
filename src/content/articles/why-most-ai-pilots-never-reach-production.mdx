## The demo is the easy part

At Codexier AB, I have seen this pattern enough times to recognize it immediately. A team builds an AI pilot, shows it in a meeting, everyone is impressed, and then three months later nobody can tell you what happened to it. The model worked. The pilot just never became a system.

The reason is almost never the model's accuracy. It is everything around the model that nobody budgeted time for.

## What actually kills pilots

A few patterns show up again and again in the projects I have worked on or watched from the outside.

- The pilot was built on clean, hand-picked data that does not look like production data.
- Nobody owns the decision to take it live, so it sits in a "let's revisit this" state forever.
- There was no plan for what happens when the model is wrong, only a plan for when it is right.
- The team measured success by demo reaction, not by a metric tied to a real business outcome.
- Integration with existing tools and workflows was treated as a detail to figure out later.

Any one of these is fixable. All five at once is why a pilot quietly disappears.

## What separates the ones that ship

The AI pilots that make it to production at Codexier AB usually have one thing in common: someone treated the pilot as a smaller version of the real system, not a separate proof of concept. The data pipeline, the error handling, and the person responsible for the outcome were defined before the first model was even trained.

It also helps to pick a narrow, boring use case first. The flashy pilots get attention. The narrow ones that solve one specific, recurring pain get renewed budget.

## The founder lesson

If you are pitching or building an AI pilot, ask before you start: who owns this after the demo, and what does "good enough to ship" actually mean in numbers. If you cannot answer both, you are building a demo, not a product. I learned this the expensive way, by building things people liked and then watched die in a backlog.
