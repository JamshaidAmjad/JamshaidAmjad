## It's less glamorous than it sounds

When people hear "Master's in Applied AI" they picture building the next breakthrough model. The reality is a lot more grounded. A large share of coursework is about cleaning datasets, choosing the right evaluation metric, and explaining results clearly enough that someone outside the field could understand them. The glamorous part of AI is a small slice of the actual work.

## The math shows up, but in service of something

There's real math: probability, linear algebra, optimization. But it's rarely taught for its own sake. It shows up because you need it to understand why a model fails in a certain way, or why one architecture handles long sequences better than another. The math is a tool for answering practical questions, not a hurdle placed in front of the interesting part.

## Group projects teach a different skill

A surprising amount of the program is group work, and the hardest part usually isn't the modeling. It's agreeing on what "done" means, splitting unclear tasks fairly, and presenting a messy result honestly instead of overselling it. These are project management skills, and they matter just as much as technical ones once you're outside school.

## The real skill being taught

If I had to name the single most valuable thing the program is teaching me, it's judgment: when a model's performance is good enough to use, when it isn't, and how to say so clearly to someone who isn't technical. Anyone can train a model. Knowing when to trust it, and when to tell a stakeholder it isn't ready, is the harder and more useful skill.

## Why I'd still recommend it

Despite the unglamorous parts, studying this formally has given me a structured way to think about problems I would have otherwise learned the hard way, through expensive mistakes on real projects.
