## Imagine you're standing on a hill in fog

You're on a hillside, it's foggy, and you can't see the bottom. Your goal is to get as low as possible. You can't see the whole landscape, but you can feel which direction is downhill from exactly where you're standing. So you take a small step in that direction. Then you check again: which way is downhill from here? You take another small step. Repeat this enough times, and you'll eventually reach the bottom of the valley, or at least a low point.

That's gradient descent. The "hill" is the error your model is making. The "downhill direction" is the gradient, a calculation that tells the model which way to adjust its weights to reduce error. The "step" is how much you change the weights each time.

## Why the step size matters so much

If your steps are too big, you might leap right over the bottom of the valley and end up on the other side, sometimes worse off than before. If your steps are too small, you'll eventually get there, but it might take forever. This step size is called the learning rate, and it's one of the first things to tune when a model trains badly. Too high, and the loss bounces around wildly instead of settling down. Too low, and training crawls.

## Why it has to repeat so many times

Each step only tells you the best direction from where you're currently standing, not the whole path to the bottom. So the model has to keep recalculating the gradient and taking a new step, over and over, often thousands of times, gradually creeping toward lower error. This is exactly why training takes time, not because the calculation at each step is hard, but because it has to be repeated patiently, one careful step after another.

## The honest takeaway

Gradient descent isn't smart in the way it sounds. It doesn't see the big picture or plan ahead. It's a patient, local, repeated process: feel the slope, take a small step, check again. That simplicity is exactly why it scales to networks with millions of weights.
