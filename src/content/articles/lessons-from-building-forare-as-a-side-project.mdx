## A side project needs its own rules

Forare started as a mobility platform concept I worked on alongside studying and running Codexier AB, and it forced me to relearn what "done" means. A full-time company can absorb a feature that takes a month. A side project cannot. I had to redefine progress on Forare as small, working pieces, like a basic dispatch logic prototype or a clean data model for trip matching, instead of waiting for a complete platform vision to take shape before building anything.

## Short sessions beat long ones

The biggest practical lesson from Forare was about time, not technology. I do not have long uninterrupted stretches for it between coursework and client work. What worked was committing to short, frequent sessions, often less than an hour, focused on one specific piece of the system. Long gaps between sessions on Forare always cost me more time relearning context than the work itself took. Consistency mattered more than session length.

## Separating the interesting problem from the useful one

Intelligent dispatch and routing are genuinely interesting problems to me, and it was easy to spend time on the parts of Forare that were intellectually fun rather than the parts that would actually validate whether anyone wanted the platform. I had to keep asking myself whether I was working on Forare because it moved the idea forward, or because it was a fun distraction from harder, more boring validation work. Being honest about that distinction kept the project from becoming an expensive hobby.

## Why I keep it as a side project

Forare has stayed deliberately scoped as a concept I develop alongside everything else, not a company I have committed to full time. That has been the right call so far. It lets me explore a real problem in mobility without forcing a decision I am not ready to make, and it keeps the pressure low enough that I actually enjoy working on it instead of resenting it.
